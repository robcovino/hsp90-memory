# Discrete gating modes and a slowly wandering closing rate generate memory in Hsp90 dynamics

Roberto Covino · 2026-10-15 · extended abstract (live version in Claude Docs). Figure: `figures/paper_figure.{pdf,png}`
(scripts `ppc_paper_figure.py`, `plot_paper_figure.py`; data `results/audit/ppc_paper.npz`).

The molecular chaperone Hsp90 opens and closes continuously, and the sequence of its open and closed dwells remembers the past for minutes. Plasmon rulers allow us to follow a single Hsp90 dimer for hours [1]. In four apo yeast molecules recorded for 6 h each at 10 Hz, the two states are separated by 11 noise standard deviations, so the 25,571 dwells are known without error, and successive open times stay correlated over 50–70 cycles. However, two very different hidden variables can produce such memory. The molecule could switch among a few discrete gating modes with fixed rates, or its rates could fluctuate continuously, as in dynamic disorder. Model-free tests detect memory without telling the two apart [2]. Here, we decided between them with the exact likelihood of the dwell sequence.

The likelihood of any kinetic scheme with rate matrix Q is a product of blocks of exp(QΔt), with Δt = 0.1 s, and is exact also for events shorter than a frame. A slow coordinate z that modulates the rates becomes part of such a scheme once z is put on a grid. In other words, continuous disorder keeps an exact likelihood. We let z relax in a harmonic well with time τw and multiplied every closing rate by exp(σz). Closing shifts the well by σ, which preserves detailed balance (Fig. 1a, b). We compared models by maximum likelihood, Bayesian evidence and leave-one-molecule-out prediction, and reproduced the likelihoods and the evidence with independent code starting from the raw counts.

Among ten discrete schemes, the best has five gating modes, ten states and 24 rate constants (Fig. 1a). Adding the slow coordinate costs two parameters, raises ln L by 77.5 and ln Z by 72, and improves the prediction of molecules left out of the fit by 76. The closing rate wanders by about 20% (σ = 0.22, 90% interval 0.19–0.26) over τw = 5 min (3–7.5 min), comparable with the lifetimes of the main gating modes. We found the same σ and τw on four different discrete skeletons, and a continuous z beats a two-state switch with as many parameters by 10 in ln L. The model reproduces the dwell-time distributions, the memory between successive dwells and the Chapman–Kolmogorov divergence (Fig. 1c–e), and corrects the local transition rates that the discrete modes miss (Fig. 1f). Dwell by dwell, the effect is very weak. Since z is nearly frozen over some forty cycles, two open times in the same mode correlate only through the z they share:

    ρ = (e^{σ²} − 1) / (2e^{σ²} − 1) ≈ σ² ≈ 0.05

Only the full likelihood of 25,000 dwells detects such a weak correlation. Our earlier simulation-based inference on summary statistics missed it and favored the discrete model.

Three observations limit the interpretation. One of the four molecules shows no modulation (held-out Δln L = −7), and five of 48 half-hour segments carry 71% of the gain, more than in any data set simulated from the fitted model. Both models make the local closing rate too persistent: in the data, its correlation is gone after 6–8 min, below the 95% predictive band (Fig. 1g). Removing all one-frame dwells lowers the gain from 78 to 48. Fixed rates within a handful of discrete modes are therefore ruled out, but σ and τw are effective parameters, not yet properties of a physical coordinate. Because the likelihood stays exact for any slow coordinate on a grid, the same analysis applies directly to more molecules and to the ATP-driven traces, where it will tell whether the wandering belongs to the protein and how energy input reshapes it.
